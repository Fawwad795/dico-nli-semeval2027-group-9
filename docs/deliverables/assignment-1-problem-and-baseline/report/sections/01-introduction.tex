\section{Introduction and Task Definition}
\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{The task as a black box.}
DiCo-NLI \citep{dico-nli-2027-task} is four-way classification over ordered pairs of short phrases. The input is a premise $p$ and a hypothesis $h$, about three words each. The output is one label from $\mathcal{L}$: \EQ{} (the phrases mean the same thing), \FW{} ($p$ entails $h$), \BW{} ($h$ entails $p$) or \NO{} (none of these; \cref{tab:task}). Take ``A young boy'' and ``A boy''. The first entails the second, so the ordered pair is \FW{}, and the same two phrases in the other order are \BW{}. The organisers build that reversal into the data. Every reversible source pair occurs twice, once in each order, and a fixed operator ties the two gold labels together: \EQ{} stays \EQ{}, \FW{} and \BW{} swap. Three scores are reported side by side. Weighted F1 is the usual per-instance score. \SoftCons{} asks whether a system's two answers on a pair obey the reversal operator, and \HardCons{} whether both of them are correct; \cref{sec:evaluation} gives the formulas. A system sees only the two strings and their language codes. The instance and pair identifiers are bookkeeping, and as we explain below they have to stay out of the model.

\paragraph{The task as a white box.}
Underneath, the task is about meaning containment. In natural logic, adding a restrictive modifier narrows a phrase, and the narrower phrase entails the broader one \citep{maccartney-manning-2009-extended}; ``on a red couch'' entails ``on a couch''. So the cues that ought to matter are which phrase carries the extra material, whether the two heads match, and what quantifiers, numerals and prepositions do to the scope of a phrase. The same structure also opens a shortcut. If the more specific phrase is usually the longer one, then length and word containment predict direction without any semantics at all, and \cref{sec:eda} shows that in this data they do.

Three further things shaped how we set the experiments up. The instance identifier leaks the label, because only reversible pairs carry a \texttt{\_\_flipped} twin, so no model input may include it. About a fifth of the development phrases also occur somewhere in training. And the release does not say whether a pair came from an image caption or a news headline, so we cannot stratify by source.

\paragraph{Why the task is hard.}
There is very little to go on. Three words, and no sentence around them. Telling equivalence from entailment often needs lexical knowledge (is ``a chocolate bar'' the same as ``a candy bar'', or a kind of one?). Negatives are frequently a single swapped word in an otherwise identical frame (``off Mexico'' against ``on Mexico''). And consistency is a property of two predictions that a classifier makes separately, with no idea that they belong together.

\paragraph{Question and findings.}
Our question is whether systems reach directional consistency by representing the relation between the two phrases, or by picking up surface asymmetries, and how much of a fine-tuned encoder's three scores a cue-only model recovers. We fixed three hypotheses before running anything. H1: a cue-only model reaches a large share of the encoder's \SoftCons{}, because each of its cues is either symmetric or flips sign exactly under reversal. H2: the encoder's advantage concentrates where the cues are uninformative, including the boundary between equivalence and entailment. H3: accuracy and consistency trade off in models trained on single ordered instances. The first two held. The third held inside our classical baselines and failed for the encoder, which changes what Assignment 2 should aim at.
