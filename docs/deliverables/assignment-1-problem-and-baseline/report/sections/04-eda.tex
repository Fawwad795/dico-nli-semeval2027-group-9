\section{Exploratory Data Analysis}
\label{sec:eda}

We analyse the training split only and choose six feature groups, each to answer one question about the task (\cref{tab:eda-groups}). Lexical-richness measures such as TTR, MTLD and HD-D are left out: on phrases of about three words they are undefined or unstable. All seven figures of the analysis are collected, uncaptioned, in the separate EDA PDF; two appear below.

\paragraph{Length.}
\textit{Observation:} the premise is longer in 78\% of \FW{} instances and the hypothesis in 78\% of \BW{} instances, while \EQ{} splits evenly (36\% premise longer, 29\% equal, 36\% hypothesis longer) and half of the \NO{} instances (49\%) have equal length (\cref{fig:length}). \textit{Interpretation:} direction leaks through form, because the entailing phrase is usually the more specific one with an added modifier; this is a shortcut a model can learn without semantics. \textit{Implication:} length belongs in the cue baseline, and every model should also be scored on instances where length points the wrong way or nowhere (\cref{sec:error}).

\paragraph{Lexical overlap and containment.}
\textit{Observation:} mean word-set Jaccard overlap is 0.41 for directional pairs against 0.24 for \EQ{} and 0.27 for \NO{}. Every hypothesis word occurs in the premise in 45\% of \FW{} instances and 2\% of \BW{} instances, and the mirror holds; the last word is shared in 58\% of directional instances (\cref{fig:lexical}). \textit{Interpretation:} directional pairs are mostly one phrase with words added or removed around a kept head, while equivalences are paraphrases with little literal overlap. \textit{Implication:} containment in both orientations is a strong but partial direction cue, firing on fewer than half of the directional pairs; overlap signals relatedness, not equivalence.

\paragraph{Semantic similarity.}
\textit{Observation:} cosine similarity of MiniLM sentence embeddings \citep{reimers-gurevych-2019-sentence} has median 0.76 for \EQ{}, 0.77 for both directions and 0.64 for \NO{}; 52\% of directional and 50\% of \EQ{} instances lie at or above the \EQ{} median, against 27\% of negatives. Cosine is symmetric, so a pair and its reversal get the same value. \textit{Interpretation:} similarity separates negatives from related pairs moderately and \EQ{} from entailment not at all, and it carries no information about direction. \textit{Implication:} a symmetric representation cannot recover direction, so we leave cosine out of the cue set and expect the encoder, which reads the ordered pair, to be needed for the equivalence boundary.

\paragraph{Linguistic and task-specific cues.}
\textit{Observation:} the two heads share a lemma in 52\% of directional instances against 30\% of \EQ{}; the part-of-speech pattern is identical in 31\% of \NO{} instances against 7\% of directional ones; the premise of a \FW{} pair carries 0.33 more adjectives on average. Numerals occur in 18\% of directional instances, named entities in 47\%, while quantifiers (3\%) and negation (under 1\%) are rare. \textit{Interpretation:} negatives are typically a single substitution inside an unchanged frame, and adjectives are the material that narrows a phrase. \textit{Implication:} head and modifier comparisons matter more than global similarity; quantifiers and negation are too rare to train on and serve as error-analysis slices instead.

\paragraph{Readability.}
\textit{Observation:} syllables per word range from 1.53 to 1.60 and characters per word from 4.5 to 4.9 across labels, and median Flesch reading ease from 77.9 to 82.4. \textit{Interpretation:} the phrases are uniformly short caption and headline language; Flesch assumes sentences and here tracks syllable density only. \textit{Implication:} readability adds nothing to separate the labels and stays out of the feature set.
