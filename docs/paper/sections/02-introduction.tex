\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Natural language inference is usually scored one pair at a time. SemEval-2027 Task 2, DiCo-NLI \citep{dico-nli-2027-task}, adds a second requirement. A system reads two short phrases in a fixed order and chooses one of four relations: the phrases are equivalent, the first entails the second, the second entails the first, or neither holds. The same pair then appears reversed, and the two answers must be compatible: a forward entailment has to become a backward one. The official scorer reports weighted F1 for the labels, \SoftCons{} for the share of reversible pairs answered compatibly, and \HardCons{} for the share answered correctly in both orders.

Consistency is easy to reach for the wrong reason. A system that answers \EQ{} for every instance is perfectly self-consistent and almost always wrong: on the development set it scores \SoftCons{} 1.000 at weighted F1 0.110. % experiments/2026-09-29-trivial-baselines-track1/results.md
A consistency score therefore says little unless correctness is read beside it. The organizers' pilot study found decoder language models to be the least self-consistent model family on this kind of data \citep{apaolaza-larraya-etal-2026-assessing}, and our own fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-base encoder \citep{he-etal-2023-debertav3}, at weighted F1 0.807, still answers 32 to 37 of the 277 reversible development pairs incompatibly, depending on the seed. % experiments/2026-09-29-deberta-base-track1/findings.md

We ask whether the reversal rule can be built into a small decoder, and what that buys. Our method, reversal-equivariant LoRA (\cref{sec:method}), adapts a frozen Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct model with low-rank adapters. It enforces the rule twice: a training loss ties the prediction for a pair to the prediction for its reverse, and at inference the two orders are averaged.

\paragraph{Research question.} Can a small decoder language model adapted with LoRA reach a fine-tuned encoder on weighted F1 and \HardCons{}, and does enforcing the reversal rule, through a training loss and through symmetrized decoding, improve correctness or only self-consistency?

\paragraph{What this paper reports.} We run the method and its comparison systems on Track 1 (English) with three seeds each: 32 GPU jobs, covering the untrained decoder, LoRA at five values of $\lambda$, and three ablations that test the treatment of negative pairs, the answer format and the model size (\cref{sec:setup}). The consistency weight is chosen on validation data and committed before any development score of those runs is read. Every comparison is decided by a rule fixed in advance, with paired bootstrap intervals and McNemar tests per seed (\cref{sec:results,sec:analysis}). We then count the errors of the main systems by category (\cref{sec:errors}), compare with the baselines and with published work (\cref{sec:comparison}), and report results that go against the hypotheses with the same weight as those that support them (\cref{sec:discussion}).
